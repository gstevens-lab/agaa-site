Leaving Your School District? What to Do With Your 403(b)

By Greg Stevens · 4 min read

Short answer: once you leave the district, you can usually leave your 403(b) where it is, roll it into an IRA, roll it into a new employer's plan, or cash it out. A direct rollover keeps it tax-deferred. Cashing out is almost always the most expensive choice.

When you can move it

You can generally move your 403(b) after you separate from the district. Many plans also let you move money at age 59½ even while you're still teaching. Plan rules vary, so check yours.

Five mistakes that cost teachers money

  • Ignoring surrender charges. Many Texas 403(b)s are annuities. If yours is still in its surrender period, moving it early can cost you. Get the schedule first.
  • Taking a check instead of a direct rollover. A check paid to you triggers 20% federal withholding. A direct rollover from company to company avoids it.
  • Losing the age-55 rule. If you leave the district in or after the year you turn 55, you can take money from that 403(b) without the 10% early-withdrawal penalty. Roll it into an IRA and that break goes away until 59½. If you'll need the money before then, keep at least part of it in the 403(b).
  • Forgetting an outstanding loan. An unpaid 403(b) loan can become a taxable distribution when you leave. Deal with it before you move the money.
  • Leaving old accounts scattered. Teachers who changed districts often have small 403(b)s in several places. Pulling them together makes them easier to manage, and easier to handle when required withdrawals start at 73 (75 if you were born in 1960 or later).

When moving makes sense

It's often worth moving if your account is past its surrender period and stuck at a low minimum rate, if it charges high fees, or if you want options it doesn't offer, like guaranteed lifetime income.

When leaving it alone is smarter

If your 403(b) has a strong guaranteed rate, if you're still inside the surrender period, or if you'll rely on the age-55 rule, staying put can be the better move. Sometimes the best advice really is leave it where it is.

Don't forget the 457

If your district offered a 457(b), it has its own advantage: after you leave the district, withdrawals from a governmental 457(b) have no 10% early-withdrawal penalty at any age. That makes it a useful bridge if you retire before 59½.
